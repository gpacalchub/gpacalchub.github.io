How GPA works

Your GPA — grade point average — is a single number that summarises how you performed across all your courses. Every letter grade is worth a set number of grade points: an A is worth 4.0, a B is worth 3.0, a C is worth 2.0, a D is worth 1.0 and an F is worth 0. Plus and minus grades sit in between, so a B+ is worth 3.3 and an A− is worth 3.7. Because courses carry different credit hours, the average is weighted: a grade in a 4-credit course moves your GPA more than the same grade in a 1-credit lab.

The maths is simple once you see it written out. For each course, multiply the grade points by the credit hours to get that course's quality points. Add up all the quality points, add up all the credit hours, and divide:

GPA = total quality points ÷ total credit hours

Here is a quick example. Suppose you earn an A (4.0) in a 3-credit English course and a B (3.0) in a 3-credit history course. Your quality points are (4.0 × 3) + (3.0 × 3) = 12 + 9 = 21, over 6 total credits, giving a GPA of 21 ÷ 6 = 3.50. Try it in the calculator above — it should match exactly. Our full walkthrough, how to calculate your GPA step by step, covers longer transcripts, repeated courses and pass/fail classes.

One important detail: a semester GPA only describes a single term, while your cumulative GPA (CGPA) averages every graded term you have completed. Colleges, scholarship committees and employers usually look at the cumulative figure, which is why the calculator above has a dedicated cumulative mode.

What counts as a good GPA?

It depends on what you are aiming for. A 3.0 (a B average) is the most common benchmark: it keeps you eligible for most scholarships, internships and graduate programmes. A 3.5 and above is generally considered strong, and a 3.7+ puts you in honours territory at many schools. Below 2.0, most colleges place students on academic probation. But context matters — a 3.2 in a demanding engineering programme can be more impressive than a 3.6 in a lighter course load. Our article on what a good GPA really is breaks the numbers down by goal: staying eligible, keeping a scholarship, applying to graduate school, or landing a first job.

How to raise your GPA

Raising a GPA is a maths problem before it is a motivation problem. Your cumulative average is anchored by every credit you have already completed, so the more credits you carry, the harder each new grade pulls. A first-year student can transform their average in one strong semester; a final-year student with 100 credits banked will see it move by hundredths. That is not a reason to give up — it is a reason to plan. Use the cumulative mode above to test scenarios: enter your current GPA and credits, then experiment with the grades you would need this semester to hit your target.

The strategies that actually work are unglamorous: prioritise high-credit courses when you allocate study time, because they carry the most weight; fix the one or two courses dragging your average down rather than spreading effort evenly; retake a failed or very low course if your school replaces the grade; and talk to instructors early, before a bad midterm becomes a bad final grade. We go deeper — including how to recover from a genuinely bad semester — in how to improve your GPA: practical strategies that work.

GPA vs CGPA: what is the difference?

The terms get mixed up constantly. A semester GPA (sometimes just called GPA) covers one term. A cumulative GPA, or CGPA, covers everything so far — it is the weighted average of all your semester GPAs, weighted by credits. Some schools use "GPA" to mean the cumulative figure and "term GPA" for the single semester, so always check which number a form is asking for. The full explanation, with worked examples of how one bad semester echoes through a CGPA, is in GPA vs CGPA: what's the difference?

Planning ahead: what grades do you need?

The most common question students ask is not "what is my GPA?" but "what do I need from here?" The cumulative calculator answers it by trial: set a target, then adjust this semester's grades until the result reaches it. If the target requires straight As in every course, the honest answer is that it is out of reach this term — and knowing that early lets you set a two-semester plan instead. Remember too that a single semester rarely defines a transcript. Admissions officers and employers both pay attention to trends, and an upward trend tells a story that a flat average cannot.

Frequently asked questions

Does an A+ count for more than an A? On the standard unweighted scale, no — both are worth 4.0. Some high schools award 4.3 for an A+ on a weighted scale, which is covered in our scale guide.

Do pass/fail courses affect my GPA? Usually not. A "pass" typically earns the credits without grade points, so it neither helps nor hurts the average — but a fail in a pass/fail course sometimes does count as an F. Check your school's policy.

How many decimal places should I use? Most transcripts report GPA to two decimal places (for example, 3.47). This calculator rounds the same way.

How to Calculate Your GPA, Step by Step

GPACalcHub · A complete walkthrough, from letter grades to your final average

Calculating a GPA by hand takes about two minutes once you know the method, and knowing the method matters: it lets you predict your average before grades are posted, check that your transcript is right, and plan what you need next semester. Here is the whole process, with nothing skipped.

Step 1: Convert each grade to grade points

Every letter grade maps to a number of grade points on the 4.0 scale. An A is 4.0, A− is 3.7, B+ is 3.3, B is 3.0, B− is 2.7, C+ is 2.3, C is 2.0, C− is 1.7, D+ is 1.3, D is 1.0 and F is 0.0. If your course reports a percentage instead, convert it first using the bands in our GPA scales guide — 93% and above is a 4.0, 90–92% is a 3.7, and so on down the scale.

Step 2: Multiply by credit hours to get quality points

Courses are not all equal. A course that meets for four hours a week and carries 4 credits should influence your average more than a 1-credit seminar, and the calculation makes sure it does. For each course, multiply the grade points by the credit hours. The result is called quality points.

Quality points = grade points × credit hours

Step 3: Add everything up and divide

Add the quality points from every course, add the credit hours from every course, and divide the first total by the second:

GPA = total quality points ÷ total credit hours

A worked example

Say your semester looks like this: English Composition, 3 credits, grade A (4.0). Calculus, 4 credits, grade B+ (3.3). Biology with lab, 4 credits, grade B (3.0). Introduction to Psychology, 3 credits, grade A− (3.7). First-Year Seminar, 1 credit, grade A (4.0).

The quality points are: English 4.0 × 3 = 12.0; Calculus 3.3 × 4 = 13.2; Biology 3.0 × 4 = 12.0; Psychology 3.7 × 3 = 11.1; Seminar 4.0 × 1 = 4.0. Total quality points: 52.3. Total credits: 15. GPA = 52.3 ÷ 15 = 3.4866…, which rounds to 3.49. Enter the same five courses into the calculator on our homepage and you will get exactly that — it is a good way to confirm you have the method right.

Notice the lesson hiding in the example: the B in 4-credit Biology cost more than the A− in 3-credit Psychology gained. When you plan where to spend study time, credit hours are the multiplier on everything.

Edge cases worth knowing

Pass/fail courses

At most schools, a course taken pass/fail is excluded from the GPA entirely when you pass: you earn the credits, but no grade points enter the calculation. A fail, however, is treated as an F (0.0) at many institutions. Because policies differ, treat pass/fail as a shield for a risky elective, not a hiding place for a required course you are likely to fail.

Repeated courses

Schools handle repeats in one of three ways: replace the old grade with the new one, average the two attempts, or count both. Grade replacement is the most generous and makes retaking a failed course one of the fastest ways to lift a GPA — see our improvement guide for when it is worth doing.

Incomplete and in-progress grades

Courses without a final grade yet simply do not count. When you are forecasting, enter the grade you realistically expect rather than the grade you hope for; a forecast built on straight As tells you nothing useful.

Transfer credits

Credits transferred from another school usually arrive as credits only — the grades stay behind. Your new school's GPA starts fresh from the courses you take there, even though the credits count toward your degree.

From semester GPA to cumulative GPA

The same formula scales up. To combine semesters, you do not average the semester GPAs (that would ignore credit differences between terms); you add all quality points ever earned and divide by all credit hours ever attempted. The GPA vs CGPA guide explains this in depth, and the calculator's cumulative mode does it automatically: enter your current GPA and completed credits, add this semester's courses, and it blends them correctly.

What Is a Good GPA? A Realistic Guide

GPACalcHub · Benchmarks for eligibility, scholarships, graduate school and employers

"Is my GPA good?" is the question every student asks, and the honest answer is: good compared to what, and good enough for what? A 3.0 that keeps a scholarship is a success; a 3.6 that misses a medical school shortlist feels like a failure. This guide replaces the vague answer with the actual thresholds people use.

The rough landscape

On the 4.0 scale, a 4.0 is a perfect record of As. A 3.7–3.9 is excellent — honours level at most colleges. A 3.4–3.6 is strong and competitive for most purposes. A 3.0–3.3 is solidly respectable: a B to B+ average. A 2.7–2.9 is below average but recoverable and rarely disqualifying on its own. A 2.0–2.6 is the warning zone, and below 2.0 most colleges impose academic probation, with dismissal a real risk if it continues. The average college GPA in the United States sits around 3.1–3.3, so "average" is higher than many students expect — grade inflation over recent decades is the main reason.

Good enough to stay enrolled and eligible

The floor at most colleges is 2.0. Drop below it and you are typically placed on academic probation; financial aid eligibility also commonly requires maintaining at least a 2.0 (alongside completing enough of the credits you attempt). Student-athletes face similar minimums from their athletic associations. If you are near this line, the priority is not excellence — it is stability: fewer risky courses per term, early tutoring, and no pass/fail gambles on required classes. Our improvement strategies are written with exactly this situation in mind.

Good enough for scholarships

Merit scholarships usually start at 3.0, with the more generous awards at 3.5 and above. Renewal conditions matter as much as entry conditions: many scholarships require you to keep a 3.0 or 3.25 every term, and losing one over a 2.98 is a painful and common story. If a scholarship sets your floor, plan your semester with margin — use the cumulative calculator to see how much slack one bad course would leave you.

Good enough for graduate and professional school

Here the bar rises. Competitive master's programmes commonly expect 3.3–3.5 and up; law and medical schools are stricter still, with admitted students at sought-after schools often averaging 3.6–3.8. But admissions committees read transcripts, not just totals: a rising trend, strong grades in the subjects closest to the programme, and a good final-two-years GPA can offset a weak first year. Many applications let you explain context — a semester disrupted by illness or work is understood when the semesters around it are strong.

Good enough for employers

Most employers who set a GPA cutoff for new graduates use 3.0. Some competitive firms in finance and consulting screen at 3.3–3.5 for first-round interviews. After your first job, the number fades fast — experience, references and work samples take over, and within a few years almost nobody asks. If your GPA is below a cutoff, leave it off the résumé rather than rounding it up; a discovered exaggeration costs far more than a modest number.

Why context changes everything

A GPA is not a pure measure of ability. Grading standards differ between schools and between departments — averages in some engineering and science programmes run half a point lower than in others, and admissions officers know their local landscape. A weighted high-school GPA above 4.0 is not "better than perfect"; it reflects honours and AP bonuses, as explained in our scales guide. Judge your number against the threshold you actually face, keep it moving in the right direction, and remember it is one line of your record — not the whole record.

How to Improve Your GPA: Practical Strategies That Work

GPACalcHub · The maths of recovery, and the habits that actually move the number

Advice about raising your GPA tends to split into two useless halves: vague motivation ("just work harder") and panic. What actually helps is understanding the arithmetic first, then aiming your effort where the arithmetic says it counts.

First, understand the maths of recovery

Your cumulative GPA is a weighted average of everything you have completed, so its responsiveness depends on how many credits sit behind it. A student with 15 credits and a 2.6 who earns a 3.8 semester jumps to roughly 3.2. A student with 90 credits and a 2.6 who earns the same 3.8 semester moves to about 2.77. Same effort, very different visible result. This is not unfairness; it is what an average is. The practical conclusion: set targets in terms of semester GPA (which you fully control) and let the cumulative number follow. Run your own scenarios with the cumulative mode of our calculator — seeing the real ceiling for this term stops you chasing an impossible target and burning out.

Prioritise by credit weight

When time runs short — and it always does around midterms — allocate study hours by credit value and by how close the grade is to a boundary. Lifting a 4-credit course from B to A adds 4 quality points to your record; lifting a 1-credit course the same way adds 1. Likewise, a course sitting at 89% is one good assignment away from an A− band, while a course at 71% needs a transformation. Work the boundaries of your heavy courses first.

Fix the drag, don't polish the strengths

Students naturally study what they enjoy, which usually means polishing A courses that are already As. Your GPA improves fastest at the bottom of your grade list. One F or D in a 3-credit course erases the benefit of several As; identifying your single worst course and getting it to a B− is worth more than nudging two As into A+s that count exactly the same on an unweighted scale. If a course is heading for failure, talk to the instructor and your advisor before the withdrawal deadline — a W on a transcript is almost always better than an F in the average.

Retake strategically

If your school replaces grades on retakes, repeating a course you failed or nearly failed is the single most powerful move available: replacing an F (0.0) with a B (3.0) in a 3-credit course adds 9 quality points while adding zero new credits to the denominator. If your school averages attempts instead, the gain is smaller but still real. Check the policy before you pay for the retake — the three common policies are compared in our calculation guide.

Habits that show up in the grade book

None of this is glamorous, which is precisely why it works. Attend every class — attendance correlates with grades more reliably than almost any study technique, partly because instructors test what they emphasise in the room. Start assignments the week they are set, not the week they are due, so a single bad week cannot cascade. Use office hours early: instructors are far more helpful about a confusing topic in week three than about a failing grade in week thirteen. And sleep — trading sleep for revision the night before an exam reliably lowers performance on exactly the kind of recall and reasoning the exam tests.

Recovering from one bad semester

A single disastrous term — illness, family trouble, a job that swallowed your hours — damages the number, but it does not have to define it. The recovery pattern that works: reduce next term's load slightly so every course gets real attention, avoid stacking your hardest required courses into the same term, and document what happened if you will later need to explain the transcript (many graduate applications provide space for exactly this). An upward trend after a bad semester is a story admissions officers and employers respond to; it demonstrates the thing a flat transcript cannot — that you can recover. For the benchmarks you are recovering toward, see what a good GPA is.

What doesn't work

Overloading on easy electives to pad the average fools no one and delays your required courses into a brutal final year. Cramming everything into finals week cannot move grades that are 70% decided by then. And obsessing over the cumulative figure daily adds stress without information — check it at midterm and at term's end, plan, and then go and do the work.

GPA Scales Explained: 4.0, 5.0, Weighted and Unweighted

GPACalcHub · Why GPA numbers are not comparable until you know the scale behind them

A GPA is meaningless without its scale. A 3.8 on a 4.0 scale is excellent; a 3.8 on a 5.0 scale is mediocre. Yet students compare numbers across schools constantly, usually wrongly. This guide explains the main systems and how to translate between them honestly.

The standard 4.0 unweighted scale

The most common system in US colleges and many high schools. Every A is worth 4.0 regardless of the course's difficulty, Bs are worth 3.0, and plus/minus grades fill the gaps (A− 3.7, B+ 3.3, and so on). The maximum possible GPA is 4.0. "Unweighted" simply means a grade in an advanced course counts the same as the same grade in a standard course. The calculator on this site uses this scale, as does the conversion table on our homepage.

Weighted scales and the 5.0 GPA

Many high schools weight grades to reward harder courses: an A in an honours class might be worth 4.5 and an A in an Advanced Placement (AP) or International Baccalaureate (IB) class worth 5.0, while a regular-class A stays at 4.0. On such a scale the maximum is 5.0 (or higher), and a weighted GPA of 4.4 is possible. Colleges know this and typically recalculate applicants' GPAs on their own unweighted basis for comparison — which is why a weighted number should never be compared directly with an unweighted one.

The percentage scale

Some schools and many countries grade in percentages and convert. The standard US conversion bands: 93–100% = 4.0, 90–92% = 3.7, 87–89% = 3.3, 83–86% = 3.0, 80–82% = 2.7, 77–79% = 2.3, 73–76% = 2.0, 70–72% = 1.7, 67–69% = 1.3, 65–66% = 1.0, below 65% = 0.0. Note the quirk: the scale is not linear. Moving from 89% to 90% gains 0.4 grade points, while moving from 94% to 99% gains nothing. Other countries use entirely different bands, so a converted GPA always carries the fingerprints of the system that produced it.

Other systems you may meet

The UK classifies degrees (First, Upper Second/2:1, and so on) rather than averaging grade points; a rough equivalence puts a First around a 3.7–4.0 and a 2:1 around 3.0–3.6, but these are approximations, not conversions. Many European universities use the ECTS A–F scale alongside national marks. India's CGPA is commonly reported on a 10-point scale, where a rough US equivalent is obtained by dividing by 9.5 and multiplying by 4 — again, an estimate that institutions treat cautiously. Pakistan, Canada and Australia each have their own percentage-to-GPA conventions, and Canadian schools vary by province and institution. Whenever a conversion matters — for an application, say — use the receiving institution's published table, not a generic one.

How to convert without fooling yourself

Convert course by course, never the final average: convert each grade to the target scale first, then take the credit-weighted average, exactly as described in our step-by-step guide. Averaging first and converting second produces a different (and usually flattering) number, because the bands are uneven. And always report the scale alongside the number — "3.6 / 4.0" is honest; a bare "3.6" invites misunderstanding. For how the resulting number stacks up, read what counts as a good GPA.

GPA vs CGPA: What's the Difference?

GPACalcHub · Semester averages, cumulative averages, and which one people actually ask for

Students use "GPA" and "CGPA" interchangeably, and then get confused when a form asks for one and their transcript shows the other. The distinction is simple, but it has real consequences for how you plan and how your record is judged.

The short answer

A semester GPA (also called term GPA) is your average for one semester or term only. A cumulative GPA — CGPA — is your average across every graded semester you have completed, combined. Your CGPA is the number that appears on your official transcript as your overall standing; your semester GPA is a snapshot of one chapter of it.

How a CGPA is actually built

A CGPA is not the average of your semester GPAs. It is calculated from raw totals: all quality points you have ever earned, divided by all credit hours you have ever attempted (the formula itself is in our calculation walkthrough). The difference shows when semesters carry different loads. Suppose semester one was 12 credits at a 3.0 GPA (36 quality points) and semester two was 18 credits at a 3.6 (64.8 quality points). Averaging the two GPAs gives 3.3, but the true CGPA is (36 + 64.8) ÷ 30 = 3.36 — the heavier semester rightly pulls more weight. The cumulative mode of our calculator handles this correctly: give it your current CGPA and credits, add the new semester, and it blends by credit weight.

Why one bad semester echoes

Because the CGPA accumulates, early semesters never disappear — they dilute. A first semester at 2.0 across 15 credits contributes 30 quality points permanently; even three later semesters at 3.8 cannot fully erase it, though they can lift the average to a respectable level (in that scenario, about 3.35 after 60 total credits). This is why advisors push students to protect their first year, and why the recovery strategies in our improvement guide emphasise semester targets over cumulative ones: the semester number is the lever, the CGPA is the gauge.

The other averages on your transcript

Two relatives are worth knowing. A major GPA counts only courses in your major or programme — graduate schools and technical employers often care about this one more than the overall figure, and a student with a 3.1 overall but a 3.6 in-major is in better shape than the headline suggests. A term or yearly GPA groups semesters by academic year and is what many scholarship renewals actually check. If a form just says "GPA", it almost always means the cumulative figure unless it specifies a term.

Which one do colleges and employers want?

The CGPA, nearly always — it is the stable, comparable number. But readers of transcripts look at the semester pattern too: steady improvement, a single explained dip, or strength in relevant subjects can all outweigh a slightly higher but flat CGPA. Keep both numbers healthy if you can; if you cannot, make the recent semesters the strong ones. For the thresholds each audience uses, see what a good GPA looks like.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions students ask most about GPA. For the full method, start with how to calculate your GPA.

How is GPA calculated?

Convert each letter grade to grade points (A = 4.0, B = 3.0, and so on), multiply by the course's credit hours to get quality points, add up all quality points, and divide by total credit hours. The calculator on our homepage does this instantly.

What is the difference between GPA and CGPA?

A semester GPA covers one term. A cumulative GPA (CGPA) covers all terms combined, weighted by credits. See GPA vs CGPA explained for a worked comparison.

Does an A+ count as more than 4.0?

Not on the standard unweighted scale — A+ and A are both 4.0. Some high schools use a weighted scale where an A+ in an advanced course can be worth 4.3 or 5.0. See GPA scales explained.

Do pass/fail courses count toward GPA?

Usually a pass earns credits without grade points, so it does not change your GPA. A fail in a pass/fail course is counted as an F (0.0) at many schools. Policies vary — check your student handbook.

What happens to my GPA if I retake a course?

It depends on your school's policy: some replace the original grade, some average both attempts, and some count both. Grade replacement makes retakes the fastest way to repair a GPA.

Do transfer credits affect my GPA?

At most institutions, transferred credits count toward your degree but the grades do not transfer — your GPA at the new school is built only from courses taken there.

What GPA do I need to keep my scholarship?

Common renewal thresholds are 3.0 or 3.25, but they are set by each scholarship's terms. Check your award letter, and plan with margin using the cumulative calculator.

How do I convert a percentage to a GPA?

Use the standard bands: 93–100% = 4.0, 90–92% = 3.7, 87–89% = 3.3, 83–86% = 3.0, 80–82% = 2.7, 77–79% = 2.3, 73–76% = 2.0, 70–72% = 1.7, 67–69% = 1.3, 65–66% = 1.0, below 65% = 0.0. The calculator's percentage mode applies these automatically. Full details are in the scales guide.

Is a 3.5 GPA good?

Yes — a 3.5 is a strong average that satisfies most scholarship, internship and graduate-school expectations. Read what is a good GPA for benchmarks by goal.

Does GPACalcHub store my grades?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser; nothing you type is sent to or stored on any server. See our privacy policy.
